Texas IN Action (TIA) — Complete Assignment Guide

DRAFT FOR INSTRUCTOR REVIEW

Texas IN Action is a menu-based assessment system for GOVT 2306 (Texas Government). Instead of high-stakes exams, students earn 100 points through authentic engagement with Texas state and local government — the commissioners courts, special purpose districts, school boards, appraisal districts, and utility authorities that shape daily life in Texas but that most Texans never see.

How TIA Works

  • 100 points required (40% of course grade)
  • 39 assignments across three effort tiers — 555 total points available, so students have enormous choice
  • Category requirement: Students must complete activities from at least two different effort tiers (Higher Effort, Medium Effort, Quick Engagement)
  • Complete/Incomplete grading: Each assignment has a checklist rubric. Meet every criterion → COMPLETE → full points. Miss any criterion → INCOMPLETE → specific feedback and one resubmission within one week
  • Activities submitted after the final deadline (Week 7) cannot be resubmitted

The Three Tiers

Tier Point Range Assignments Character
Higher Effort 20–30 pts 12 Field work, investigations, interviews
Medium Effort 10–15 pts 13 Focused research, observations, journals
Quick Engagement 5–8 pts 14 Reflections, short videos, rapid lookups

A Note for Students Who Have Never "Done" Local Government

That's most of you — and that's the point. Every assignment below includes a "How to Get Started" section that walks you through exactly where to look, what website to use, and what to expect when you show up. Nobody is born knowing how a commissioners court works. By the end of this project, you'll know more about how Texas actually governs itself than most lifelong residents.

Academic Integrity & the AI Question

Every TIA assignment requires your specific details: your address's districts, your county's officials, your photos, your tax bill, your conversations. Generic AI-generated text cannot complete these assignments because the rubrics demand verifiable local specifics and original documentation. Submissions containing fabricated local details, fake officials, or AI-generated content presented as field work receive an INCOMPLETE and a referral under the college's academic integrity policy.

Points: 30 Estimated Time: 4–5 hours Best For: Students curious why their tax bill has so many lines on it

Assignment Description

Texas has more than 3,000 special purpose districts — invisible governments that can tax you, charge you fees, take on debt in your name, and hold elections you've probably never voted in. Municipal Utility Districts (MUDs), Emergency Services Districts (ESDs), hospital districts, college districts, water districts, Public Improvement Districts (PIDs) — they layer over your home address like transparent sheets, and most Texans cannot name a single one they live in.

Your mission: map every special purpose district that covers your home address, find out what each one charges you, who governs it, and whether you've ever had a say.

How to Get Started

  1. Pull your property tax statement (or your family's, or your landlord's — for renters, your county appraisal district website lets you look up any address's taxing entities for free). In Tarrant County, go to TAD.org, search the address, and look at the "Taxing Entities" or "Jurisdictions" list.
  2. For each entity that isn't the city, county, or ISD, look it up in the Comptroller's Special Purpose District Public Information Database (spdpid.comptroller.texas.gov). It lists board members, contact info, tax rates, and outstanding debt — all self-reported under state law.
  3. Read pages 1–10 of "Invisible Government: Special Purpose Districts in Texas" (Texas Senate Research Center — free PDF, linked from comptroller.texas.gov/transparency/local/special-purpose.php).
  4. If your address has few or no special districts (it happens), expand to your whole ZIP code or compare your address with a relative's or friend's address elsewhere in DFW.

Requirements

  • Identify every taxing/fee-charging district covering your chosen address (minimum 4 entities total, including city/county/ISD if needed to reach 4)
  • For each special purpose district: its purpose, governing board, current tax rate or fee structure, and outstanding debt (from SPDPID or the district's site)
  • Calculate the combined annual cost of these districts for the property (use the appraisal district's value)
  • Find out when the district's board elections are held and who is eligible to vote in them
  • Written report (800 words minimum) including a "what surprised me" analysis
  • Screenshot evidence from the appraisal district lookup and SPDPID

Complete/Incomplete Rubric

Students earn COMPLETE (30 points) when ALL criteria below are met:

District Mapping ✓

  • A real, specific address is used (street name may be redacted in the report; instructor verification available on request)
  • Every taxing entity for that address is identified (minimum 4)
  • Each special purpose district's type and legal purpose is correctly described
  • Screenshot from county appraisal district jurisdiction lookup included
  • Screenshot from SPDPID entry (or district website) for at least one district included

Financial Analysis ✓

  • Current tax rate or fee structure documented for each district
  • Combined annual cost calculated using the property's actual appraised value
  • Outstanding debt reported for at least one district (or documented as zero/unreported)
  • Math is shown and correct

Governance Analysis ✓

  • Board members or governing structure identified for at least two districts
  • Election timing and voter eligibility researched and reported
  • Report addresses whether the student (or property owner) has ever voted in these elections

Written Report (800 words minimum) ✓

  • Meets word count
  • "What surprised me" section shows genuine engagement, not generic statements
  • Connects findings to course concepts (special districts, local government fragmentation, accountability)
  • Specific names, numbers, and rates appear throughout (not vague summaries)

INCOMPLETE Status: If any criteria are not met, student receives specific feedback and may revise within one week.

Points: 25 Estimated Time: 3–4 hours (including meeting time) Best For: Students who want to see Texas's most misunderstood government in action

Assignment Description

Despite the name, a commissioners court is not a court. It is the governing body of every one of Texas's 254 counties — a county judge plus four commissioners who together control the county budget, set the county tax rate, run the jail and county roads, oversee elections, and manage emergency response. It is arguably the most powerful local government you've never watched.

Attend (in person or via official livestream/recording) one full commissioners court meeting and analyze what you saw.

How to Get Started

  1. Find your county's commissioners court schedule. Tarrant County Commissioners Court typically meets Tuesday mornings — check tarrantcountytx.gov for the calendar, agendas, and the official video stream/archive. (Dallas, Denton, Johnson, and Parker counties all post theirs too — use your home county.)
  2. Download the agenda before you watch. Agendas are posted at least 72 hours in advance under the Texas Open Meetings Act. Skim it and pick 2–3 items to track closely.
  3. In person? Meetings are public — you can walk in. Arrive early, sit anywhere in the gallery, silence your phone. Streaming? Live or archived official video both count, but you must watch a complete recent meeting (within the current semester).

Requirements

  • Attend or stream one complete commissioners court meeting from the current semester
  • Proof of engagement: a photo of you at the meeting OR timestamped screenshots from the stream at three different points
  • The agenda (saved/linked) with your 2–3 tracked items marked
  • Written analysis (700 words minimum) covering: who the county judge and commissioners are (by name and precinct), what was decided on your tracked items, how the meeting blends legislative and executive functions, and one budget or tax item discussed
  • Identify which commissioner represents your precinct

Complete/Incomplete Rubric

Students earn COMPLETE (25 points) when ALL criteria below are met:

Attendance & Documentation ✓

  • Complete meeting from current semester attended or streamed
  • Photo at meeting OR three timestamped screenshots provided
  • Agenda included with tracked items clearly marked
  • Meeting is a genuine commissioners court (not city council or other body)

Knowledge of the Court ✓

  • County judge and all four commissioners named correctly
  • Student's own precinct and commissioner identified
  • The court's hybrid legislative/executive nature explained with a specific example from the meeting

Analysis (700 words minimum) ✓

  • Meets word count
  • 2–3 tracked agenda items summarized with outcomes
  • At least one budget, contract, or tax-related item analyzed
  • Connects to course concepts (county government structure, plural executive at the local level, Open Meetings Act)
  • Includes specific quotes, votes, or moments only attainable by actually watching

Assignment Description

Texas is in the middle of a data center boom — and DFW is its epicenter. These facilities consume enormous electricity and water, receive local tax incentives, and are reshaping the grid so fast that the Legislature passed Senate Bill 6 (2025) giving ERCOT new powers over "large loads," including a remote-disconnect requirement critics call the "kill switch." Meanwhile, data centers are excluded from the state's flagship JETI school-tax incentive — but can still receive local Chapter 312 property tax abatements and a state sales tax exemption.

Investigate one real data center project in Texas (DFW alone offers dozens) and produce an impact report: who approved it, what incentives it received, what it demands from the grid and water supply, and who bears the costs and benefits.

How to Get Started

  1. Pick a project. Search local news (Fort Worth Report, Dallas Morning News, Star-Telegram, Dallas Business Journal) for "data center" plus a city name — Fort Worth, Arlington, Lancaster, Red Oak, Garland, and Abilene have all had major announcements. Pick one with public records.
  2. Find the local government trail. Search the city council and/or commissioners court agendas/minutes for the project or company name. Look for zoning cases, Chapter 312/380 agreements, water/infrastructure deals.
  3. Check the incentive databases. The Comptroller's Chapter 312 abatement and Chapter 380/381 agreement databases (comptroller.texas.gov → Transparency → Local) list registered agreements.
  4. Understand SB 6. Read at least one substantive explainer on SB 6's requirements for 75 MW+ loads (interconnection fees, disclosure, remote disconnect, curtailment).

Requirements

  • One specific, named, real Texas data center project documented
  • The local approval trail: which government bodies voted on what, and when (agendas/minutes cited)
  • Any incentives identified (Chapter 312/380, sales tax exemption eligibility) — or documented absence of incentives
  • Estimated power and/or water demand from credible reporting
  • Explanation of how SB 6 applies (or doesn't) to this facility
  • Written report (900 words minimum) with a balanced costs/benefits assessment for the host community
  • Minimum 5 credible sources, cited

Complete/Incomplete Rubric

Students earn COMPLETE (30 points) when ALL criteria below are met:

Project Documentation ✓

  • A real, specific Texas data center project is the subject
  • Local government approval trail traced with at least one agenda, minutes document, or official record cited
  • Approving bodies correctly identified (city council, commissioners court, school board, etc.)

Incentives & Law ✓

  • Incentive status researched and accurately reported (Chapter 312/380, sales tax exemption, JETI exclusion noted)
  • SB 6's relevance explained correctly (75 MW threshold, disclosure, remote disconnect/curtailment)
  • Distinction between state and local incentive authority is clear

Impact Analysis ✓

  • Power and/or water demand figures included from credible reporting
  • Benefits to host community identified (tax base, jobs, infrastructure)
  • Costs/risks identified (grid strain, water, noise/land use, abatement revenue foregone)
  • Assessment is balanced, not one-sided

Report Quality (900 words minimum) ✓

  • Meets word count
  • Minimum 5 credible sources cited
  • Connects to course concepts (local economic development, special districts/utilities, state regulatory power)
  • Specific dollar figures, dates, and names throughout

Assignment Description

Texas law guarantees your right to address local governing bodies. Deliver a public comment — on an issue you actually care about — at a city council, school board, or commissioners court meeting in Texas.

How to Get Started

  1. Pick your body and issue. Fort Worth City Council, your home city's council, your ISD's board, or your county's commissioners court. Pick an issue genuinely connected to your life: streets, housing, school policy, parks, public safety, library hours — anything within that body's actual authority.
  2. Learn the sign-up procedure. Every body posts its public comment rules: some require online sign-up by a deadline (Fort Worth uses an online speaker card system), some have sign-up sheets at the door. Time limits are typically 2–3 minutes. Call the city secretary's office if the website is unclear — that phone call is itself civic engagement.
  3. Write and rehearse. 2–3 minutes is roughly 250–350 words. State who you are, what you're asking for, and why — respectfully and specifically.
  4. Anxiety accommodation: If speaking is a genuine barrier, written comments formally submitted into the record (where the body accepts them) qualify — document the submission.

Requirements

  • Research the issue with at least 2 credible sources
  • Written transcript of your prepared comment (within the body's time limit)
  • Deliver the comment at a legitimate Texas governing body meeting
  • Proof: video clip, meeting video timestamp, sign-in confirmation, or official minutes listing you
  • Reflection (500 words minimum): the procedure you navigated, how officials responded, what you'd do differently

Complete/Incomplete Rubric

Students earn COMPLETE (25 points) when ALL criteria below are met:

Preparation ✓

  • Issue researched with minimum 2 credible sources
  • Issue falls within the body's actual jurisdiction
  • Comment transcript provided, within time limit, respectful and specific
  • Clear position or request stated

Delivery & Documentation ✓

  • Comment delivered at a legitimate Texas government body meeting (or written comment formally submitted with documentation, if accommodation applies)
  • Proof of participation provided
  • Sign-up/registration procedure described accurately

Reflection (500 words minimum) ✓

  • Meets word count
  • Describes officials' response (or lack thereof)
  • Evaluates accessibility of the public comment process honestly
  • Connects to course concepts (participation, representation, responsiveness)

Assignment Description

Texas school finance is famously labyrinthine: a "basic allotment" the Legislature raised by only $55 in 2025 (the first increase since 2019), a "recapture" system (nicknamed Robin Hood) that pulls money from property-wealthy districts, billion-dollar local bond elections, and now school-district tax abatements for industry. Pick one Texas ISD you have a personal connection to and follow its money.

How to Get Started

  1. Pick your ISD (where you attended, where your kids attend, or where you live — e.g., Fort Worth ISD, Crowley ISD, Arlington ISD, Keller ISD).
  2. Find the budget. Every ISD posts an adopted budget and tax rate on its website (search "[district name] adopted budget"). TEA's website also publishes district financial data.
  3. Check recapture status. Search "[district] recapture" or look in the budget documents — does your district pay into recapture or receive state aid?
  4. Find the last bond election. Search "[district] bond election" — what was proposed, what passed, what's the debt?

Requirements

  • One specific ISD with your stated personal connection
  • The district's current total tax rate, broken into M&O and I&S components, explained in plain English
  • The district's per-student funding picture, referencing the basic allotment and HB 2 (2025) changes
  • Recapture status documented (payer, recipient of state aid, or neither — with numbers if available)
  • The most recent bond election: amount, purpose, result, current outstanding debt
  • Written report (800 words minimum) answering: "Who really funds my district, and who decides?"

Complete/Incomplete Rubric

Students earn COMPLETE (25 points) when ALL criteria below are met:

District Facts ✓

  • Specific ISD identified with genuine personal connection stated
  • Current tax rate found and correctly split into M&O and I&S
  • Budget document or TEA data cited (link or screenshot)

Finance System Understanding ✓

  • Basic allotment and HB 2 (2025) changes accurately described
  • Recapture explained correctly and district's status documented
  • State vs. local funding shares addressed

Bond & Debt ✓

  • Most recent bond election documented (amount, purpose, outcome)
  • Outstanding debt reported or documented as unavailable

Report Quality (800 words minimum) ✓

  • Meets word count
  • Central question answered with evidence
  • Connects to course concepts (fiscal federalism within Texas, local control, school governance)
  • Real numbers from the actual district throughout

Assignment Description

Interview two different people who work in or for Texas local government — elected or staff. Justices of the peace, constables, city council members, school trustees, city planners, county clerks, election administrators, firefighters in an ESD, librarians, code compliance officers — the menu is enormous, and most are far more accessible than students expect.

How to Get Started

  1. Aim local and aim realistic. A school board trustee or JP is far more reachable than the mayor of Fort Worth. Official emails are public on city/county websites. A polite email mentioning you're a TCC government student requesting 15–20 minutes works remarkably often.
  2. Two different roles — ideally one elected official and one government employee, or two different levels (city + county).
  3. Prepare 6–8 questions per interview about their actual work: What does your office really do? What do residents misunderstand? How does state law constrain you? What decision are you proudest of?
  4. Phone, video, or in-person all count. Record only with permission, or take detailed notes.

Requirements

  • Question lists submitted for both
  • Documentation: recording (with permission), detailed notes, or confirmation emails plus notes

Assignment Description

Texas celebrates local control — except when the Legislature doesn't. In 2023 lawmakers passed HB 2127, the "Texas Regulatory Consistency Act" (opponents call it the "Death Star Bill"), sweeping away local ordinances across whole fields of law. Cities sued; a district court declared it unconstitutional; the state's appeal kept the law in effect while courts wrestle with it. And HB 2127 is just the biggest example — Texas has preempted local rules on plastic bags, fracking, short-term rentals, paid sick leave, and more.

Pick one specific state-vs-local conflict (HB 2127 itself, or a narrower fight) and analyze it from both sides.

How to Get Started

  1. Pick your conflict. Options: HB 2127 broadly; Denton's 2014 fracking ban and HB 40; the plastic bag ordinance fight (City of Laredo case); short-term rental battles; a current Fort Worth/Arlington/Dallas example from local news.
  2. Get the basics from quality sources: Texas Tribune, Texas Municipal League, court filings, the bill text on capitol.texas.gov.
  3. Find the current status — especially for HB 2127, where litigation is ongoing. Your report must state the legal status as of your submission date, with a source.

Requirements

  • One specific, named conflict with the relevant bill/ordinance/case identified
  • Accurate timeline of the conflict
  • The strongest version of BOTH arguments: state uniformity/business climate vs. home rule/local democracy
  • Current legal status as of submission, sourced
  • Connection to the Texas Constitution's home rule provision (Art. XI, Sec. 5)
  • Written analysis (800 words minimum) ending with your own assessed position

Complete/Incomplete Rubric

Students earn COMPLETE (25 points) when ALL criteria below are met:

Case Facts ✓

  • Specific conflict chosen with bill numbers/ordinances/cases correctly cited
  • Timeline accurate
  • Current legal status stated as of submission date with source

Two-Sided Analysis ✓

  • State's strongest arguments presented fairly
  • Cities'/local strongest arguments presented fairly
  • Home rule (Art. XI, Sec. 5) correctly explained and applied

Written Analysis (800 words minimum) ✓

  • Meets word count
  • Student's own position stated and defended with evidence
  • Connects to course concepts (home rule vs. general law cities, Dillon's Rule tensions, federalism analogies)
  • Minimum 4 credible sources cited

Assignment Description

Pick one bill from a recent Texas legislative session that affects local communities, and follow it from filing to real-world implementation. Because the Legislature meets in regular session only in odd-numbered years, this assignment works in any semester: in session years you can track a live bill; in interim years you trace a recently passed law's implementation (often the more interesting story).

How to Get Started

  2. Use Texas Legislature Online (capitol.texas.gov): search the bill number, read the bill analysis (plain-English summary), check the actions list (committee, votes, signing), and watch at least 15 minutes of archived committee testimony on the bill.
  3. Find the local landing. How is YOUR city, county, school district, or a local agency implementing or responding to this law? Search local news and local government agendas.

Requirements

Assignment Description

Texas has no state income tax, which means property taxes do the heavy lifting — and the system (appraisal districts, exemptions, rate-setting, truth-in-taxation hearings) is a complete mystery to most people paying it. Dissect a real property tax statement line by line, and observe rate-setting in action.

How to Get Started

  1. Get a statement. Yours, family's, or — for renters — look up your apartment complex or any address on your county appraisal district site (TAD.org in Tarrant; all records are public).
  2. Decode every line: each taxing entity, its rate, the appraised vs. taxable value, and every exemption applied (homestead, over-65, disabled veteran...). The Comptroller's property tax pages explain each exemption.
  3. Truth-in-taxation: find your county's tax rate notice site (Texas counties must post proposed rates, no-new-revenue rates, and hearing dates — many use a "tax transparency" portal). Watch or attend one tax rate hearing OR read the official notice and minutes for the most recent one.
  4. Bonus understanding: note the difference between the appraisal district (sets values) and taxing units (set rates) — most Texans conflate them.

Requirements

  • A real statement or appraisal-district record analyzed (sensitive details may be redacted)
  • Every line item explained: entity, rate, what it funds
  • Appraised vs. taxable value and all exemptions explained; if no exemptions, explain which could apply
  • Truth-in-taxation component: one taxing unit's most recent rate adoption documented (proposed vs. no-new-revenue rate, hearing date, outcome)
  • The appraisal district vs. taxing unit distinction explained correctly
  • Written report (700 words minimum) including: "If I wanted to lower this bill, what are my actual legal options?" (exemptions, protest by May 15 or 30 days after notice, showing up at rate hearings)

Complete/Incomplete Rubric

Students earn COMPLETE (25 points) when ALL criteria below are met:

Statement Dissection ✓

  • Real statement/record used (screenshot or scan, redaction allowed)
  • Every taxing entity and rate identified and explained
  • Appraised vs. taxable value distinction handled correctly
  • Exemptions explained (applied or potentially applicable)

Rate-Setting & Process ✓

  • One taxing unit's most recent rate adoption documented with proposed vs. no-new-revenue comparison
  • Appraisal district vs. taxing units distinction correctly explained
  • Protest process accurately described (deadline: May 15 or 30 days after notice; ARB; Form 50-132)

Report Quality (700 words minimum) ✓

  • Meets word count
  • "Legal options" section is accurate and specific
  • Connects to course concepts (local revenue, truth-in-taxation, no state income tax tradeoff)
  • Real numbers from the actual record throughout

Points: 25 Estimated Time: 3–4 hours Best For: Students motivated by keeping their family and neighbors safe

Assignment Description

When disaster hits Texas — tornado, flood, ice storm, grid emergency — a specific local machinery activates: the county emergency management office, city emergency plans, Emergency Services Districts, outdoor warning sirens, alert systems, and shelters. Most residents discover this machinery only mid-crisis. Audit your community's emergency preparedness infrastructure before you need it.

How to Get Started

  1. Find your county's office of emergency management (Tarrant County OEM, or your county's) and your city's emergency management page.
  2. Sign up for your local emergency alert system and document it (most Tarrant-area jurisdictions use a county/city alert registration; your county OEM page links it).
  3. Map the local hazards: what does your county's hazard mitigation plan or OEM page identify as top risks? (In North Texas: tornadoes, flash floods, winter storms, extreme heat.)
  4. Identify your nearest resources: outdoor warning sirens policy (when do they sound?), nearest designated shelter or cooling/warming station, who provides fire/EMS at your address (city department or an ESD — check your tax entities!).
  5. Build your household plan using the state's ready.texas.gov framework (TDEM).

Requirements

  • County and city emergency management offices identified with their actual responsibilities
  • Proof of alert system registration (screenshot)
  • Top 3 local hazards identified from official sources
  • Fire/EMS provider for your address identified (city dept. or named ESD)
  • Outdoor warning siren policy for your jurisdiction explained
  • A completed written household emergency plan (TDEM/ready.texas.gov framework)
  • Reflection (600 words minimum): what existed that you didn't know about, and one gap you identified

Complete/Incomplete Rubric

Students earn COMPLETE (25 points) when ALL criteria below are met:

Government Infrastructure Mapping ✓

  • County OEM and city emergency management correctly identified with roles
  • Fire/EMS provider for student's address correctly identified
  • Siren policy accurately described for their jurisdiction
  • Top 3 hazards drawn from official local sources

Assignment Description

The Texas Constitution of 1876 has been amended over 500 times and contains genuine oddities. Find one specific provision or amendment that strikes you as strange, hyper-specific, or surprising, and explain it.

How to Get Started

  1. Browse the Texas Constitution on the Legislature's statutes site (statutes.capitol.texas.gov) or the Legislative Reference Library's amendment history.
  2. Good hunting grounds: Article III's pages of specific exceptions; Article XVI's miscellany; recent amendment elections (each November of odd years brings a fresh crop of hyper-specific propositions).
  3. Pick ONE provision; quote or accurately paraphrase it; find out WHY it's in there (amendment history, news coverage of the election that passed it).

Requirements

  • One specific constitutional provision quoted/cited accurately (article, section, or amendment year)
  • The backstory: why it exists, when adopted, what problem it solved (or created)
  • Explanation of why such detail lands in the CONSTITUTION rather than statute (the statute-like constitution problem)

Points: 6 Estimated Time: 45 minutes Best For: Students preparing for a Higher Effort meeting assignment — or anyone curious

Assignment Description

Local government agendas are public, posted 72 hours ahead under the Texas Open Meetings Act — and written in a dialect of bureaucratese. Find one upcoming agenda (city council, school board, or commissioners court near you) and translate three items into plain English.

How to Get Started

  1. Find the "Agendas & Minutes" page for any local body near you (cities and counties almost all use portals like Legistar, CivicClerk, or PDF postings).
  2. Pick an agenda for a meeting that hasn't happened yet.
  3. Choose 3 items — at least one involving money — and decode them: what is actually being decided, who's affected, what would a YES do?

Requirements

  • Screenshot/link of an upcoming (not past) agenda from a body near you
  • 3 items translated to plain English (one involving money/contracts)
  • For each: what's being decided, who's affected, and one question a resident should ask
  • Note the Open Meetings Act posting requirement and where you found the posting

Complete/Incomplete Rubric

Agenda Work ✓

  • Genuine upcoming agenda from a real nearby body, documented
  • 3 items decoded accurately, including one financial item
  • Translations are plain-English and correct

Civic Literacy ✓

  • A resident-question posed for each item
  • Open Meetings Act posting requirement correctly noted
